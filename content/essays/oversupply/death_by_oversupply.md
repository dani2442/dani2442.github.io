> What do *books, art, music, video games, mathematics* have in common?

I remember that the last time I went to the cinema and was genuinely impressed by a movie's visual effects was *Avatar* (2009). Since then, I feel that visual effects have stagnated and won't improve much more. The same applies to video games: the last big leap was *Red Dead Redemption 2* (2018), or perhaps *GTA VI*. Few people expect another order-of-magnitude jump anytime soon.

Returning to the question above, the answer is that none of them can become outdated or used up. In some cases, the opposite is true: they can become more valuable with time (classic literature and art are the obvious examples). This has a catastrophic implication for their respective industries: effective supply increases over time, while demand stays relatively constant, or even declines (for example, due to falling birth rates in most developed countries).

#### New creators have the Herculean task of competing with the entire history of human creativity.

AI may postpone this problem for a while by increasing diversity and lowering costs, but the underlying dynamic remains.

You may argue that I am being too pessimistic, so let's consider some counterarguments:

- **Discovery is limited**: Most people can't access the "entire history" meaningfully. However, recommendation systems and search engines keep getting better at surfacing relevant content.
- **Novelty and social value**: This is the strongest counterargument, but I believe novelty also has limits.
